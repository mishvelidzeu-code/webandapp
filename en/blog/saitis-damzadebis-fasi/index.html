How much does a website cost in Georgia in 2026?

Website development cost in Georgia: what determines the price, what should be included, what the hidden costs are and how to compare agency offers fairly.

Published: 2026-09-12 · 4 min read

“How much does a website cost?” is the question we hear most often — and the hardest to answer with a single number. Instead of a number, this article gives you a tool: what creates the price, what should be inside it, what tends to be left out, and how to compare two different offers fairly.

In short: starting prices by type

A website’s price is set first by its type and scope. These are webandapp’s starting prices — the point an offer begins from, with features added on top:

For comparison: in September 2026 public offers from Georgian agencies and freelancers list multi-page sites from roughly GEL 200 to 1,500 and online stores often from GEL 2,000–2,500. These are “from” prices — a real project usually starts above them, which is exactly why it pays to understand what stands behind the number.

What determines the price

  • Number and type of pages. Five similar service pages and five completely different sections (catalogue, vacancies, gallery, blog, calculator) do not cost the same. Price follows the number of unique templates, not the raw page count.
  • Languages. Each additional language means separate addresses, full translation, titles and descriptions. A Georgian + English + Russian site is noticeably more work than one language, even with a single design.
  • Content management system. If you need to edit text yourself, you need WordPress or another CMS — with extra configuration, security and maintenance. A static site that rarely changes is cheaper and faster.
  • Integrations. Online payment, a booking calendar, CRM, couriers, accounting — each is separate work and often depends on third-party terms.
  • Design originality. Adapting a ready theme and a design built from scratch with illustrations, animations and unique components are different budgets.

Secondary but real factors are migrating content from an old site (with redirects so Google rankings survive), copywriting, photography and product data preparation — these often sit outside the offer and are assumed to be on the client’s side.

What should be included

When comparing offers, check that these points are explicitly included — if any is missing, the “cheap” offer ends up expensive:

  • Planning of page structure and user journey (not only “design”)
  • A mobile version checked on real phones
  • A contact form that actually arrives in your inbox, plus call/WhatsApp buttons
  • Technical SEO foundations: titles, descriptions, sitemap, speed, Search Console
  • HTTPS and basic security
  • Publishing on your domain, with domain and hosting registered in your name
  • A handover walkthrough — how to change text, whom to contact with a problem

Hidden and running costs

  • Domain — an annual fee that varies by registrar. Always in your name.
  • Hosting — depends on type: some platforms offer free plans for static sites (check the commercial-use terms), WordPress needs PHP hosting, a store needs more.
  • Paid licences — theme, plugins, fonts, photos. A good offer lists them separately.
  • Payment-provider fees — a percentage of transactions for an online store.
  • Maintenance — updates and backups if you do not do them yourself; practically mandatory for WordPress.

How to compare two offers

  1. How many unique pages/templates are included and what happens if I need more?
  2. In whose name are the domain, hosting and admin accounts registered?
  3. What is not included? (copy, photos, licences, translation, integrations)
  4. What happens after launch — who fixes a bug and how fast?
  5. Can I receive the code and materials if the collaboration ends?
  6. What are the stages and when do I see intermediate results?

If an offer does not answer these questions, the price is not a price yet — it will change later.
